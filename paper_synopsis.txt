Shen et al. 2019 · Communications Biology

KIRIN1 & GINKO1
What they did
Designed the first genetically encoded fluorescent K⁺ biosensors using Kbp, a bacterial protein that changes shape when it binds K⁺. Built three indicators: two FRET-based (KIRIN1 using cyan/yellow FPs, KIRIN1-GR using green/red FPs) and one single-FP indicator (GINKO1) made by inserting intact Kbp into EGFP.

How they did it
Proteins expressed in E. coli, purified by affinity chromatography, then characterised by fluorescence titration and stopped-flow kinetics. Tested in HeLa cells (drug-induced K⁺ depletion) and rat cortical neurons (glutamate stimulation). Co-expressed GINKO1 with red Ca²⁺ indicator K-GECO1 for dual-colour imaging.

What they found
GINKO1 stood out — single FP, excitation-ratiometric, Kd 0.42 mM, ~2.5× signal change, highly K⁺-selective. Dual imaging revealed for the first time optically that neurons lose K⁺ when firing while astrocytes simultaneously take it up — two opposite roles in the same frame.

Limitation
GINKO1 responds weakly to very high Na⁺. Signal change modest. Never tested in a living animal. Authors explicitly flag that an optimised GINKO variant is the next step.


Wu et al. 2022 · PLoS Biology

GINKO2
What they did
Solved the crystal structure of GINKO1, used that structural knowledge to guide engineering of an improved sensor (GINKO2), then deployed it across bacteria, plants, and mice — the first in vivo demonstrations of a genetically encoded K⁺ sensor across multiple organisms.

How they did it
Crystal structure solved at 1.85 Å by X-ray crystallography (APS beamline, molecular replacement with COOT/PHENIX). Engineering done in two stages — structure-guided mutagenesis at key residues near the chromophore and linker regions, followed by multiple rounds of directed evolution via error-prone PCR in E. coli. In vivo: bacteria grown in K⁺-depleted media tracked by plate reader; Arabidopsis roots imaged under salt stress by spinning disk confocal with microfluidics; mouse cortex imaged by 2-photon microscopy during cortical spreading depolarisation.

What they found
GINKO2 showed 15× fluorescence increase, 20× ratiometric excitation change, no Na⁺ cross-reactivity, and 1.8× brighter than GINKO1. Structure revealed K⁺ is held by 6 backbone carbonyls — same mechanism as the KcsA K⁺ channel. In bacteria, intracellular K⁺ dropped from ~103 mM to ~20 mM under starvation. In plant roots, salt stress triggered K⁺ efflux visible in real time. In mouse brain, extracellular K⁺ waves during cortical spreading depolarisation were captured live.

Limitation
pH sensitivity (pKa 6.8) is close to physiological pH — fluorescence changes can be misread as K⁺ changes if pH shifts. Drosophila experiments were inconclusive because of this. Every application needs a parallel pH control experiment.


Ashraf et al. 2016 · Structure

Kbp — the potassium binding protein
What they did
Discovered and characterised Kbp (formerly YgaU), a small 16 kDa cytoplasmic protein from E. coli that binds K⁺ with high specificity. Showed it is required for normal bacterial growth at high K⁺ concentrations and undergoes a large conformational change upon K⁺ binding — making it a candidate cytoplasmic K⁺ sensor.

How they did it
Kbp expressed with a C-terminal His-tag in E. coli BL21(DE3), purified by Ni-affinity chromatography and gel filtration. K⁺ binding confirmed and quantified by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) — binding is enthalpy driven, single site, Kd ~160 µM. Structural changes on K⁺ binding characterised by NMR (¹⁵N-HSQC spectra), circular dichroism (far- and near-UV CD), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Solution structure of K⁺-saturated Kbp solved by NMR spectroscopy (PDB: 5FIM). Global conformational change (open → compact) confirmed by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and analytical ultracentrifugation. Kinetics measured by stopped-flow fluorescence. Domain contributions dissected by purifying isolated BON and LysM domains separately.

What they found
Kbp binds a single K⁺ ion with high selectivity over Na⁺ — K⁺ stabilises the protein from a largely disordered (apo) state into a compact globular structure. Both domains are needed: the BON domain contains the primary binding site (Kd ~20 mM alone) but the LysM domain stabilises the complex (full Kbp Kd ~160 µM). SAXS showed the protein shrinks dramatically on K⁺ binding — maximum particle diameter drops from 12.5 nm (apo, elongated) to 6.5 nm (bound, globular). Bacteria lacking Kbp grow normally at low K⁺ but show a 2–5 hour growth delay at high K⁺ concentrations, confirming a physiological role in K⁺ homeostasis.

Why it matters for the GINKO story
This paper is the foundation of everything that follows. The conformational change Kbp undergoes on K⁺ binding — disordered open to compact closed — is exactly the mechanical movement that Shen et al. 2019 and Wu et al. 2022 exploit to build their biosensors. Without this paper, there is no KIRIN1, no GINKO1, no GINKO2. The NMR structure deposited here (5FIM) was also used directly as a molecular replacement search model to solve the GINKO1 crystal structure in Wu et al. 2022.

Limitation
The exact K⁺ binding site on Kbp was not resolved — the NMR structure shows the overall fold but the specific coordinating residues remained unknown. That question was only answered when Wu et al. 2022 obtained the crystal structure of GINKO1 and saw the 6 backbone carbonyls coordinating K⁺ directly.

Abramson et al. 2024 · Nature

AlphaFold 3

What they did
Built a single unified deep-learning model capable of predicting the 3D structure of biomolecular complexes — proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules, ions, and modified residues all together — outperforming specialised tools across nearly every interaction type tested.

How they did it
Replaced AF2's evoformer with a simpler pairformer, and swapped the structure module for a diffusion module that works directly on raw atom coordinates. Trained on PDB structures up to September 2021 in three stages with increasing crop sizes. Used cross-distillation from AlphaFold-Multimer predictions to suppress hallucination in disordered regions. Developed a diffusion rollout procedure to train confidence scores. Benchmarked on PoseBusters (428 protein–ligand structures), recent PDB releases, and CASP15 RNA targets.

What they found
AF3 achieved ~76% success on protein–ligand docking without any ground-truth pocket information, far exceeding AutoDock Vina and RoseTTAFold All-Atom. Protein–nucleic acid prediction substantially surpassed RoseTTAFold2NA. Antibody–antigen and protein complex accuracy both improved over AF-Multimer v2.3. Confidence scores (pLDDT, PAE) tracked accuracy well across all molecule types.

Limitation
Chirality errors in ~4.4% of ligand predictions. Occasional severe chain clashing in large protein–nucleic complexes. Hallucination in disordered regions persists. Predicts only static conformations — always output the closed state of E3 ligases regardless of ligand presence. No code released; restricted non-commercial web server only.
